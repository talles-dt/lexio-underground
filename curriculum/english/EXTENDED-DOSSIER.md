## Documento A — Planning memorandum

**To:** Programme committee · **From:** Coordination team · **Purpose:** decision on a six-week pilot

The centre currently runs one in-person learning session on Thursday evenings. It can allocate 600 credits to a six-week pilot. The existing session is funded separately, so this budget concerns additional activity rather than the cost of keeping the centre open. We have received requests for an online option and for a Saturday session. Neither request has yet been approved. The committee must choose an option that can be delivered with the available staff, explain the basis for its choice and specify what will be reviewed before any continuation is considered.

Option One is a Saturday session. The room costs 35 credits per week and facilitation costs 40 per week, giving a six-week direct cost of 450 credits. The room has a step-free entrance. The booking includes tables and chairs but does not include additional transport or childcare. The facilitator has confirmed availability for all six weeks. A further 60 credits would cover a printed information pack for the whole pilot. This pack is optional; it is not a weekly charge. The room provider can hold the dates until Friday, after which availability must be checked again.

Option Two is an online session. Platform support costs 15 credits per week and facilitation costs 45 per week, giving a six-week direct cost of 360 credits. The proposed facilitator has confirmed only the first four weeks. Another facilitator might cover the remaining two, but this has not been agreed. Loan devices are not included. A local partner has offered to discuss a small device loan, but no number, date or eligibility rule has been confirmed. We must therefore avoid announcing that equipment will be available. The online option could include a 90-credit technical orientation session before the first meeting. That is a single additional cost, not a weekly one.

Option Three is to run both complete six-week options. Their direct costs total 810 credits before printed material or technical orientation. This is outside the available budget. A shorter combination may be proposed, but it must be costed explicitly and must not assume that the providers will accept a different booking arrangement. No additional funding is confirmed. The committee should not describe a funding request as money already available.

Whichever option is selected, we recommend collecting attendance using one consistent definition: each participant is counted once for each session in which they take part. We also need to record whether they completed the main activity, while allowing them to decline to explain why they left early. These are different indicators. A person may attend without completing an activity, or may complete it later. Any public report should state which indicator it uses. A pilot can be worthwhile even if it reveals that the chosen arrangement needs to change.

## Documento B — Consultation note

**Prepared by:** Volunteer consultation group · **Status:** descriptive account, not a representative survey

Twenty-four current participants responded to a short questionnaire during two Thursday sessions. Fourteen expressed interest in a Saturday meeting and ten expressed interest in an online meeting. Respondents were asked to select one preference, even if they would have considered both. We did not collect information from people who had already stopped attending, and we do not know how many potential participants never came to a Thursday session. These omissions matter because the purpose of the pilot is partly to widen participation beyond the current group.

The written comments do not fall neatly into two opposing camps. One person who selected Saturday explained that they needed help using online platforms. Another selected Saturday because their home was too noisy for a live call. A respondent who selected online wanted to avoid travel after work, while another was interested in recordings rather than live meetings. Recordings are not currently included in either proposal. A vote for “online” should not therefore be interpreted as agreement with every feature of the proposed online session.

We also held two informal conversations. A former participant said Thursday transport connections had made attendance difficult, but they were unavailable on Saturdays because of work. Another person welcomed the possibility of Saturday meetings but asked whether childcare would be offered. The current budget does not provide it. These conversations identify questions to investigate; they do not tell us how common either situation is. The participants gave permission for their comments to be used anonymously in this fictional exercise, but the curriculum contains no real interview data.

The consultation group recommends asking a broader set of people about specific arrangements: time, place, equipment, support and whether attendance can be occasional. We should avoid asking only “Would you attend?” without describing the actual offer. A favourable answer to an attractive but incomplete description is not a reliable booking. At the same time, consultation should not become an indefinite reason to postpone every small experiment. The committee can make a limited decision, describe whom it expects to serve, and make clear which groups it has not yet reached.

## Documento C — Measurement and communication review

**Prepared by:** Independent reviewer within the scenario · **Question:** what can be inferred from last year’s pilot?

Last year, a short online trial recorded 80 logins across four sessions, compared with 40 attendance entries across four earlier in-person sessions. A draft publicity statement described this as “participation doubled”. The calculation is simple, but the comparison is not. The login record includes reconnections after technical failures. The in-person record counts each person once per session. Neither total is a count of unique people across the entire four-week period. We cannot recover a reliable number of unique online participants from the surviving record alone.

This does not establish that the online trial failed. It establishes that the available totals cannot answer the question in the form in which it was asked. We do have six feedback messages. Four mention reduced travel, one describes repeated connection problems, and one asks for a later start. All six were sent voluntarily after the final session. They may help us identify benefits and barriers, but they cannot establish their frequency among all participants. Silence from other participants is not evidence of satisfaction, dissatisfaction or lack of interest.

The publicity statement should therefore be revised. One possible version is: “Last year’s online trial generated positive comments about reduced travel, alongside reports of connection difficulties. The attendance records used different counting methods, so they do not support a direct comparison with earlier in-person sessions.” This is less dramatic than the original headline, but it gives the audience a more accurate account of what is known. A shorter public version may be needed, provided it keeps both the benefit reported and the limitation on comparison.

For the new pilot, the reviewer recommends agreeing on definitions before the first session, checking that staff can apply them and separating attendance from completion. A brief exit question could ask what helped or hindered participation. People should be free not to answer, and the public summary should report how many responses it includes. If a decision is made to continue the pilot, the committee should explain how it weighed cost, access, feedback and operational feasibility. No single indicator will settle all four questions.

## Documento D — Briefing oral: What our numbers leave out

**Uso:** aproximadamente uma exposição curta de vários minutos; medir duração na gravação, sem impor velocidade artificial. Primeira escuta sem roteiro; segunda para conferir estrutura e condições. O leitor pode pausar naturalmente, mas não explicar respostas durante a coleta de evidência.

I want to begin with the phrase “participation doubled”, because it is the sort of phrase that makes a meeting move very quickly. Everyone feels they have been given a clear result. There is a number, a comparison and an implied direction of travel. We can almost hear the next sentence: therefore, we should do more of the same. The trouble is that the clarity of the phrase may be greater than the clarity of the evidence behind it.

In last year’s records, eighty logins were compared with forty attendance entries. Let me correct something I said in an earlier briefing: I called the eighty entries “participants”. They were logins. Some may have been the same person reconnecting. We cannot now work backwards with confidence and discover how many people there were. That correction is not a minor matter of terminology. It changes what the number can be used to claim.

Now, it would be equally unhelpful to swing to the opposite conclusion and say the trial achieved nothing. The feedback about travel was real within the scenario we are considering. Four respondents said the online format reduced that burden. For those people, the change may have been substantial. But the feedback was voluntary, and it came after the final session. The person who struggled in the first week and never returned may be missing from it. I say “may” because we should not invent that person’s experience either. Absence from our record is a reason to ask, not a licence to supply an answer.

There is a practical issue here that I do not want to lose. Staff need a system they can actually use. I once saw a registration form grow from one page to five because every department wanted one more useful detail. It became a beautifully comprehensive account of the people who had the patience to finish it. That is an exaggeration, of course, but you can see the problem. More information is not automatically better information if the process of collecting it changes who remains in view.

So, for this pilot, I would start small. Count a person once per session. Record completion separately. Ask one short question about what helped or hindered participation, and allow people to decline. Then check whether the records are being kept consistently. Incidentally, “completion” also needs a definition. Does it mean staying until the end? Finishing the main activity? Sending the work later? We should not discover in week six that three staff members have been answering three different questions.

None of this tells the committee which option to choose today. The Saturday option has confirmed facilitation and a room with step-free access, but it does not solve every transport, work or childcare problem. The online option costs less on the direct figures, yet its final two weeks of facilitation are not confirmed, and device support remains a possibility rather than a resource. We should not make either option appear universally accessible by leaving its barriers out of the description.

What we can do is make the decision proportionate. If the committee selects Saturday, it can say that it is testing a particular arrangement for six weeks, not declaring Saturday the answer for everyone. If it selects online, it needs to resolve staffing before announcing six guaranteed sessions. If it wants a shorter combination, the providers must confirm that the new arrangement is possible. The arithmetic alone does not book a room or recruit a facilitator.

One last point about communication. An update is not a decision, a proposal is not an approval, and an approval is not evidence that an activity has happened. Those distinctions sound obvious when listed like that. They become less obvious in a cheerful public announcement written at the end of a long day. My recommendation is that every announcement name the approved action, the unresolved condition and the next point at which the public will hear from us. If there is no unresolved condition, wonderful. But do not remove one merely because it makes the sentence untidy.

To return to the question we started with: what do our numbers leave out? Sometimes they leave out people. Sometimes they leave out definitions. Sometimes they leave out the uncertainty that should accompany a comparison. The response is not to abandon numbers, but to place them beside accounts of experience and clear operational facts. Then we can make a decision that is useful today and still open to correction tomorrow.

## Trabalho do aluno — separar do gabarito

1. Calcule as opções completas com seus extras opcionais. Qual cabe no orçamento sem recursos novos?
2. Distinga interesse declarado, disponibilidade real e confirmação de reserva.
3. Explique por que 80/40 não comprova o dobro de participantes.
4. Identifique duas vozes ausentes ou parcialmente representadas; não invente suas preferências.
5. Na escuta, registre a autocorreção e a função da história do formulário de cinco páginas.
6. Proponha decisão, condições, responsável por verificar e data relativa de revisão.
7. Escreva objeção forte à própria escolha e responda com evidências.
8. Prepare versão pública que não anuncie equipamento, gravações ou equipe ainda não confirmados.

## Gabarito de compreensão e limites da produção

1. Sábado: 6×(35+40)=450; com material60, total510. Online: 6×(15+45)=360; orientação90, total450. Ambos completos:810 antes dos extras; não cabem em600. Online cabe financeiramente, mas tem pendência de equipe. Não confundir viabilidade financeira e operacional.
2. Preferência do questionário não é compromisso; proposta não é reserva; sala de sábado está segurada só até sexta. Não há agenda real fora do cenário.
3. Reconexões contam na base online, não na presencial. Totais também não são pessoas únicas ao longo das semanas.
4. Exemplos: quem saiu, quem nunca compareceu, quem não usa plataforma; as duas conversas não representam todos esses grupos.
5. Participants foi corrigido para logins. A história do formulário ilustra como coleta excessiva pode selecionar quem permanece; não é estudo estatístico.
6. Não há única escolha correta. Uma recomendação de sábado deve reconhecer barreiras restantes; online exige cobrir duas semanas de facilitação; combinação curta exige orçamento e nova confirmação dos fornecedores.
7. Boa resposta enfrenta custo, acesso ou operação, em vez de caricaturar quem discorda.
8. Publicar apenas ação aprovada no role-play, limites e próximo contato. Se comissão não aprovou, chamar de proposta.
